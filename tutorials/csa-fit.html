Fit a CSA under MAS

Fit a two-site CSA to a 7Li MAS spectrum, and decide whether to believe the result.

Data used here: 7Li Hahn-echo spectrum of a lithiated Nb2O5 sample (discharged to 1.3 V), 500 MHz (194.38 MHz for 7Li), 1.3 mm rotor at 40 kHz MAS. Two lithium environments, both with a large anisotropy.

Before this, load the spectrum, then Autophase and Baseline Correct it exactly as in the previous tutorial.

1. Set up the model

  1. Select the Fit tool and open CSA, Quad & J-coupling. Leave Model on “Peaks with interactions”.
  2. Check the Nucleus (7Li) and Larmor frequency (read from the data). Set Regime to MAS (centreband + sidebands) and the MAS rate to 40000 Hz.
  3. Set the fit window to A ppm range and enter the low and high limits (here −700 and 700 ppm). Keep the spinning sidebands inside it: they carry the anisotropy.
  4. Click Add peak twice. Give Site 1 a shift of −5.7 ppm and a FWHM of 8 ppm, Site 2 0 ppm and 5 ppm. Choose pseudo-Voigt as the line shape, so each site fits its own Lorentzian fraction.
  5. With a site selected, click Add interaction ▾ and choose CSA. Start both sites from Δ = −200 ppm and η = 0.8. Euler angles stay fixed: one powder line shape rarely determines them.
  6. Tick Fit the baseline too, and tick Free on the MAS rate row of the parameter table (more on this below).

Why the MAS rate is free. The rate in the acquisition file is often stale, and a few hundred Hz of error moves every sideband. Because the sidebands are what the fit is built on, a wrong rate silently distorts Δ and η. The app warns when the sideband positions suggest the rate is off, and offers to free it. Here the fit settled at 40059 ± 10 Hz. That uncertainty is statistical only and does not include the real drift of the rotor.

2. Fit

Click Fit CSA, Quad & J-coupling. A progress window shows the fit running. When it ends, the plot shows the data, each site’s share of the fit, the baseline and the total.

3. Read the result, critically

The report lists every parameter with its 1σ uncertainty. For our spectrum:

δiso (ppm)Δ (ppm)ηFWHM (ppm)share (%)
Site 1−5.922 ± 0.016−178.7 ± 3.20.84 ± 0.077.57 ± 0.0466.6 ± 0.4
Site 2−0.245 ± 0.016−215.5 ± 4.30.83 ± 0.084.99 ± 0.0433.4 ± 0.4

R² = 0.9987, MAS rate 40059 ± 10 Hz. Δ is the Haeberlen anisotropy, δzz − δiso, and η its asymmetry. Three things to take from the report before you quote any of these numbers:

  • An R² near 1 is not the test. The report says the leftover is about three times the noise, so the model misses something real. The sideband intensities are fitted well but the numbers are the best this model can do, not necessarily the right ones.
  • η is weakly determined. At 40 kHz the pattern spreads over only about two sidebands. The app warns that you want at least 3–5. The proof is in our own runs: with the MAS rate held fixed, Site 1’s η ran to its bound (0.98); once the rate was freed it came to 0.84. Slower spinning, or a static spectrum, pins Δ and η down; a faster rate does not.
  • These are effective values. A 7Li anisotropy of −180 to −220 ppm (about 35–40 kHz at this field) is far too big for an ordinary diamagnetic lithium. In a reduced, electronically active host it can include paramagnetic and Knight-type contributions as well as the chemical shift tensor, which a single-field fit cannot separate. Report Δ as an effective anisotropy unless you have independent evidence.

What to try next

  • Show Parameter Correlations displays which parameters trade off. Δ and η of the same site usually do.
  • Change the starting values (or use Try starts in the parameter table) and confirm that you land on the same minimum.
  • Test a third site. If it is needed, the leftover will drop sharply; if it is not, it will fit a bump in the noise.
  • Record a second spectrum at a different spinning rate and fit both together (see Multi-spectrum in the Fit tool). That is the surest way to a trustworthy η.
  • Keep This Fit… stores the model under a name; Open in Simulate sends it to the simulator.
